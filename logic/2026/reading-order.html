How to read the book

Velleman writes Chapters 1 and 2 with logic and set theory braided together, and he does it on purpose. Chapter 1 is called “Sentential Logic,” but §1.3 and §1.4 are about sets; Chapter 2 is called “Quantificational Logic,” but §2.3 is about sets again.

The reason is that sets are the best available worked example of the logical connectives. “x is in A or x is in B” is a disjunction. “x is in A and not in B” is a conjunction with a negation. And the fact that A ∪ (B ∩ C) = (A ∪ B) ∩ (A ∪ C) is nothing but the distributive law for ∧ and ∨ with “x ∈ —” wrapped around every letter. Velleman introduces a connective and then immediately shows you a set operation that is that connective in disguise.

I am going to separate them. We will do all of the propositional logic first, then all of the predicate logic, and only then all of the set theory. Both orders are legitimate and they cover exactly the same material. Here is what each looks like, so you can follow whichever suits you.

The tradeoff is real and worth stating plainly. You will meet the ten equivalence laws in §1.2 and then wait two or three weeks before using them for anything, which is less satisfying than Velleman's version. What you get in exchange is that each of the three ideas gets a clean, uninterrupted run — and when the set operations do arrive, you already have the full logical toolkit, and you get to watch the whole of §1.4 fall out of laws you already know cold. Sets stop being a parallel topic and become the first application.

One thing we borrow early. Section 2.1 uses the abbreviations ∀x ∈ A and ∃x ∈ A, so we need the single symbol ∈ (“is an element of”) before we build any set theory. Take it on faith for three weeks: A is a collection of things, and x ∈ A means x is one of them. That is the only thing we borrow.

What “bound” means

A variable is bound when some operator has captured it, and free otherwise. In ∀x (x > 0) the x is bound by the quantifier. In x > c, both letters are free. In {x ∈ ℝ | 13 − 2x > c} the x is bound by the braces and the c is free.

Here is the test that matters: renaming a bound variable changes nothing. {x | P(x)} and {y | P(y)} are the same set. ∫f(x) dx and ∫f(t) dt are the same integral — you have been using bound variables since Calculus I without anyone naming them. Renaming a free variable, on the other hand, changes what you said. Free variables are the slots you can still put values into; bound ones are already spoken for.

A note on when we cover it. Velleman first explains free and bound variables using set-builder notation, because the x in {x | P(x)} is his example of a bound variable. We will meet binding through quantifiers instead — ∀x and ∃x bind x in exactly the same way — and then meet set-builder notation in Unit III as a second thing that binds a variable. If you are reading §1.3 straight through and hit set-builder notation before we have covered it, that is why.

If you prefer Velleman's order, read the book straight through. That is entirely legitimate. Every problem I assign is labeled with its section number, so either way you will know where to find it.

Why does he say “sentential” and I say “propositional”?

Same subject, two names. A sentence is a piece of language; a proposition is supposed to be the thing a sentence expresses — so that “it is raining” and “il pleut” are two sentences expressing one proposition. Logicians who would rather not commit to propositions existing as objects in their own right say sentential logic, because all they need is sentences and the operations that build new sentences out of old ones. Mathematicians and computer scientists mostly say propositional logic and don't worry about it. Velleman is writing in the logicians' idiom; I grew up in the other one.

The same thing happens one chapter later. Velleman's quantificational logic is what most people call predicate logic, or first-order logic. All three names mean the logic you get when you add ∀ and ∃ to the propositional connectives. If you go looking for other sources — and you should — you will find them filed under the other names.

Sections we skip, and the exercises that go with them

We do not cover §4.4 (ordering relations), §5.4 (closures), §6.2, §6.5 or Chapter 7 (number theory). Chapter 4 is taught only as far as equivalence relations require. Congruence modulo m is done inside §4.5, so you get modular arithmetic without Chapter 7.

§5.5 (images and inverse images) and Chapter 8 (infinite sets) are covered. Both were off the list in earlier drafts of this course and are now on the schedule — §5.5 on November 18, and Chapter 8 in the last three meetings of the term, after induction. If you saw an older version of this page saying otherwise, this one is right.

Separately, a handful of individual exercises inside sections we do cover quietly depend on sections we skip. They are not harder — they use vocabulary you will not have. These are single exercises, not whole sections; everything else in each of these sections is assigned as normal.

  • In §4.5 — skip exercises 25, 26 and 27. 25 and 26 ask about antisymmetry and partial orders on a quotient; 27 puts a partial order on the set of partitions and asks in part (c) for a greatest lower bound. All three are §4.4.
    (§4.5 has 27 exercises in the 3rd edition. An earlier version of this page said “30”, which is a 2nd-edition number and does not exist in our book.)
  • In §5.1 — skip exercise 19(b) only. That part needs partial orders (§4.4).
    Do 19(a) and 19(c). The stem of #19 is where big-oh is defined — §5.1 is the only place in the book that defines it — and 19(a) is the concrete verification against that definition (that 7x + 3 is big-oh of x2 but not conversely). You need both later: §6.3 #13 and #21 and §6.4 #15 and #16 all use big-oh. 19(c) is good practice. An earlier version of this page told you to skip all of 19 — don't.
  • In §5.2 — skip exercise 22(b) and 22(g). Largest element of a quotient under an ordering (§4.4). The other parts of 22 are fine.

That is the complete list, and it is shorter than it used to be. Every entry on it is there for one reason: §4.4, ordering relations. Everything else in §§1.1–3.7, 4.1–4.3, 4.5, 5.1–5.3, 5.5, 6.1, 6.3, 6.4 and 8.1–8.3 is fair game.

Nothing in §6.1 is skipped. An earlier version of this page told you to skip §6.1 exercise 7 because it was about images and inverse images. It isn't — #7 asks you to find and prove a formula for 30 + 31 + ··· + 3n, which is pure induction. Do it. Nothing in §6.1 depends on anything we skip.

One thing that looks like it should be on the list and isn't: several §6.4 exercises talk about the smallest element of a set. That is the well-ordering principle, which we do cover in §6.4 — it is not the order theory of §4.4. Do those.

Problems

These are not collected and not graded. They are the course. Quiz problems look like these, and there is no other way to get good at this than doing them on paper with the book shut. Starred problems are the ones I am most likely to work in class or ask about on a quiz. (There are no exams in this course — the Friday quizzes are the whole grade.)

Unit I — Propositional Logic

  • §1.1: 1, 3, 5, 6, 9★
    3 is the Alice-and-Bob problem — four negations, two of which are secretly the same. It is the one that kills “not both” versus “both not.” 9 identifies premises and conclusions; do it together with §1.2 #7.
  • §1.2: 3, 5, 7, 11★, 13, 15, 18
    11 is the first proof-shaped writing in the course: prove equivalences by citing laws rather than by building a table. 18 asks what you can conclude about an argument whose conclusion is a tautology — short, and better than it looks.
  • §1.5: 3★, 6, 7, 11★, 12
    3 is the best converse-versus-conditional problem in the chapter. Do 11. We cite it by name in Unit III.

Unit II — Predicate Logic

  • §1.3: 1★, 2 — these two only; the rest of §1.3 waits for Unit III
  • §2.1: 1, 3, 4, 6, 7★
    3 is our free-versus-bound drill. 7 is five formulas about parents and children that differ only in the order of the quantifiers — this is the ∀∃ versus ∃∀ problem and it is worth an afternoon.
  • §2.2: 1(a)(b)(d), 2(a)(b), 3, 4★, 5, 6, 7★, 9★, 15
    4 derives one quantifier-negation law from the other in three lines. 7 is genuinely surprising and reuses 6 as a lemma. 9 is the first counterexample you will construct.

Unit III — Sets

  • §1.3: 4, 5, 7, 9
    9 ends with a nested set-builder expression — that is where you find out whether you know what the braces do.
  • §1.4: 1, 3, 5★, 9★, 10, 15
    5 is the bridge problem of the whole block: prove set identities by translating to ∈-statements and citing the laws from §1.2 by name. This is the moment Unit I pays off. 10 is the repair half: (A ∪ B) \ B is not A in general, and 10(b) asks you to prove what it always is. If you want the counterexample half as well, do 11 — that is the one that asks whether (A \ B) ∪ B = A, and then what is true instead. Counterexample-then-repair is the habit Chapter 3 will demand.
  • §2.2: 11★, 13
    11 shows set theory is logic with ∈ in it. 13 is the one that cites §1.5 #11.
  • §2.3: 3, 6, 9★, 12, 15, 16
    3 asks for the power set of {∅}, and half of you will get one element instead of two. 16 is Russell's paradox: the very first thing you learned in Unit I is what breaks naive set theory in the last section of the block.
